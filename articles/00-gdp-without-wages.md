# GDP without wages: who buys what the machines make?

*A research follow-up to [AI needs a customer](https://krystofmincev.substack.com/p/ai-needs-a-customer). Draft, 10 October 2026. All model quantities are invented examples; a model period is not a calendar year.*

A machine arrives in a small town. Every business can make twice as much. Nobody gets a pay rise. Later, the machine takes the jobs too.

The town can still grow under some assumptions. Owners can buy consumer goods and businesses can buy equipment. Falling prices can make unchanged money income buy more. But higher GDP can coexist with former workers consuming nothing. The answer about production does not settle the answer about people's lives.

In *AI needs a customer*, I asked how extraordinary investment in AI would become sustainable customer demand. This study follows that question through a small, reproducible economy. It separates what machines can produce, what people and businesses buy, how purchases are financed, and who receives the output.

## A town whose accounts add up

There are ten firms, ten workers and ten owners. Two firms operate at each stage: raw materials, processing, intermediate goods and services, consumer goods, and equipment. Workers can divide their time across firms. Each employed worker earns 6 per period.

There is no government, foreign trade or borrowing. Prices initially equal one. The firms have enough capacity to meet baseline demand. Neither competition nor investment decisions are optimized; the spending and technology rules are assumptions we can change.

The town initially produces 100 of final output. Workers receive 60 and owners receive 40. Workers consume their entire income. Owners consume half theirs, or 20. Businesses buy 20 of newly produced equipment:

**GDP 100 = worker consumption 60 + owner consumption 20 + investment 20.**

Total household consumption is 80. Worker consumption is only one part of it.

Firms also sell inputs to one another. Their combined turnover is 169.4, but 69.4 consists of intermediate purchases. Subtract those purchases and value added is 100. Adding every invoice would count parts of the finished goods repeatedly. This is the [standard value-added definition of GDP](https://www.bea.gov/help/faq/1197).

Here owner income means gross operating surplus, before subtracting worn-out capital, taxes or interest. It is not automatically spendable net profit. The initial spending comparison leaves depreciation aside; the later growth experiment makes it explicit.

## Twice the capacity need not mean twice the sales

First, double productive capacity. Keep wages, prices, spending habits and equipment purchases unchanged.

Actual GDP stays at 100. The town can produce more but has no additional orders. Firms produce to order in this version. Producing unsold goods would initially add inventory investment, but accumulating inventories is a different assumption from finding lasting customers. [BEA explains inventory accounting](https://www.bea.gov/resources/methodologies/nipa-handbook/pdf/chapter-07.pdf).

Now let every price fall by half, with sufficient capacity available. The same 100 of spending buys 200 units of output. Workers buy 120 units, owners buy 40, and businesses buy 40 of equipment.

**Nominal GDP stays at 100. Real GDP doubles.** No nominal pay rise is required.

This assumes that consumption and equipment prices fall together. Actual rents, food, debts and software prices need not move together. It establishes a possibility, not the likely path of the cost of living.

It also shows why a maximum needs conditions. If physical capacity is 200, real output cannot exceed 200 in that period. Demand may keep it below that ceiling. A different technology or capital stock creates a different ceiling. “Ten workers” alone gives us neither a universal maximum GDP nor a maximum growth rate.

## One layoff, then three, five and ten

Return prices to one. Automation preserves capacity as employment falls. Owners still consume half their income; businesses still purchase 20 of equipment. After income and spending have adjusted, the model gives:

| Workers losing wages | Worker consumption | Owner consumption | Total consumption | Investment | GDP |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 60 | 20 | 80 | 20 | 100 |
| 1 | 54 | 20 | 74 | 20 | 94 |
| 3 | 42 | 20 | 62 | 20 | 82 |
| 5 | 30 | 20 | 50 | 20 | 70 |
| 10 | 0 | 20 | 20 | 20 | 40 |

The arithmetic follows directly from the spending rules. Let the wage bill be W, investment be I, and GDP be Y. Owners receive Y minus W. Then:

**Y = W + 0.5 × (Y − W) + I**, which gives **Y = W + 2I**.

Each lost wage of 6 therefore reduces GDP by 6 at unchanged investment. An additional 3 of equipment purchases offsets that loss under these assumptions, provided production and financing are available.

Owners' aggregate income remains 40 throughout the table. Lower payroll saves firms money, but workers' lower spending reduces their sales. Here the eventual sales loss absorbs the aggregate payroll saving. Other spending rules produce different profit outcomes.

At zero wages, GDP is still 40. Owners consume 20 and firms invest 20. **Zero worker consumption is not zero total consumption.**

The former workers remain in the town. They have lost their claim on output. GDP of 40 does not describe the severity of that outcome for them.

A falling labor share is also different from falling labor income. In the BLS US nonfarm business series, labor compensation's share declined from 62.8% in 2000 Q4 to 52.8% in 2026 Q2. This includes more than wages and does not cover the whole economy. [BLS, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/labor-share-at-its-lowest-level-52-8-percent-in-second-quarter-2026.htm). If output grows sufficiently, labor income can rise despite a smaller share. This historical comparison does not identify AI's effect or forecast its future.

## Restoring GDP and restoring access are different tasks

In the zero-job case, raise investment from 20 to 50. GDP returns to 100: owners consume 50 and firms invest 50. Total consumption remains below the original 80, and former workers still consume nothing.

Alternatively, keep investment at 20 and transfer 60 from owners to the former workers. GDP returns to 100, consumption returns to 80, and worker households consume 60. Owners receive gross income of 100, transfer 60, and consume half the remaining 40.

The transfer creates no extra production by itself. It changes who spends income. Broader ownership offers another route: households can receive dividends without receiving wages.

These are conditional examples. They assume that transfers can be made and investment financed. They do not establish which policy is feasible or desirable. They show why employment alone leaves the outcome unfinished.

## Can machines keep the economy growing without human work?

Zero employees would not necessarily mean zero human work if owners still managed or maintained the machines. For the stronger question, assume every required task is automated, including maintenance and building replacement machines. Energy and materials remain available on scalable terms. There is no hidden workforce abroad.

This describes a hypothetical technology, not today's AI.

In this separate growth experiment, owners start with 200 units of productive capital. Each produces half a unit of output per period, giving real GDP of 100. Ten percent of capital wears out, requiring 20 of replacement investment.

Owners consume 70 and invest 30. Capital next period is:

**200 − 20 + 30 = 210.**

The new stock produces 105. Consumption becomes 73.5 and investment 31.5. Output and consumption grow by 5% per period. After twenty transitions, real GDP is 265.33 and consumption is 185.73.

The owners buy both the consumer goods and the equipment. Worker households still receive nothing. A growing economy need not restore their access.

This example keeps nominal expenditure at 100 and assumes a common price that clears the market. By the twentieth transition, the price is about 0.377 of its starting level. It specifies an allocation people are willing to purchase; it does not prove that actual firms will find those prices. The separate cash simulation does not verify this growth example's exact payment sequence.

Replacement also matters. Initial **gross GDP is 100, but net domestic product is 80** after deducting depreciation of 20. Investment of 30 contains only 10 of net capital addition. Gross production, net production and consumption answer different questions.

If the investment share is s, output per unit of capital is a, and depreciation is d, the growth rule is:

**Growth per period = s × a − d.**

Our inputs give `0.30 × 0.50 − 0.10 = 0.05`. Investing 20% merely replaces depreciation. Investing less makes this capital stock shrink.

The conditional upper bound here is 40% growth per period: invest all output, giving `1 × 0.50 − 0.10`. But consumption is then zero. Positive consumption requires an investment share below one, and therefore growth below that bound. This is a property of the assumed linear technology, not an estimate of attainable economic growth. Scarce inputs or diminishing returns can remove the constant-growth path.

Nor have we explained why owners would want endless accumulation with no consumption. An accounting-consistent path is not necessarily an optimizing choice. GDP measures production; it does not certify usefulness, equality or welfare. [BEA's conceptual framework](https://www.bea.gov/resources/methodologies/nipa-handbook/pdf/chapter-02.pdf).

China is therefore an analogy with limits. It combines substantial investment with relatively weak household demand, but retains consumers, exports, government and finance. The [IMF's recent consultation](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/cr/2026/english/1chnea2026001-source-pdf.pdf) calls for rebalancing toward consumption. It does not describe a closed economy with no consumption.

## The money has to make the journey

The stationary table assumes investment is financeable. A payment simulation asks what happens when money must move between actual accounts.

It starts with 200 units of existing money across households and firms. Workers and owners spend from previously received income. Owners supply equity financing for equipment purchases; that financing is not counted again as GDP. Every payment has a payer and a recipient, and overdrafts are forbidden.

After all jobs disappear, inherited worker income initially supports spending. GDP follows:

**100 → 70 → 55 → 47.5 → 43.75 → … → 40.**

The total money stock stays at 200. Its distribution and rate of spending change. The transition is different from the final comparison, and its periods have no assigned calendar duration.

If owners instead stop financing investment while all workers remain employed, firms initially use cash reserves. Investment subsequently stops, capital wears out, and this particular simulation eventually cannot pay the contracted payroll. With machines that never wear out, the same financing change can settle near GDP of 60 without that payroll failure. Finance and depreciation are separate mechanisms.

In a closed economy without government, realized saving equals realized investment. That accounting identity does not guarantee that desired saving finds willing investment. Income can adjust until the realized totals match. Buying existing financial assets is also different from buying newly produced equipment.

Our payment rules are deliberately narrow. Real banks can create deposits when lending, so investment need not await a matching pile of previously saved household deposits. [Bank of England](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2014/q1/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy). Credit, policy, price changes and bankruptcy introduce additional responses.

## What this establishes, and what follows

Flat wages do not impose a fixed ceiling on real GDP. No wages need not mean no demand. Neither observation guarantees that automation will be profitable, that GDP will grow, or that most households will benefit.

AI revenue also need not equal additional GDP. Suppose final sales stay at 100,000 while a firm replaces a domestic HR invoice of 10,000 with 10 of domestic AI services. Consolidated value added can remain 100,000. The supplier mix and income distribution change; the avoided invoice is not automatically lost GDP. Cheaper services, quality measurement and the boundary around unpaid household production raise separate questions.

The baseline does not yet decide when firms automate. It gives them the technology and changes employment from outside the model. The [second experiment](03-when-the-economy-breaks.md) makes investment respond to sales, tests sudden and gradual changes, compares private automation incentives with closure, and identifies conditional payment and growth limits. It shows which assumptions drive a recovery or contraction, rather than claiming a forecast from an invented town.

Machines determine what could be produced. Spending, prices and finance determine what is realized. Pay, ownership and transfers determine who can consume it. We need all three before deciding what kind of prosperity automation creates.
