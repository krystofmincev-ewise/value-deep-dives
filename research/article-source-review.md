## Verdict

Both drafts are suitable for an Economics 101 reader. All actionable suggestions from this review are resolved in the refreshed snapshot. The drafts preserve the original's first-person, conditional voice and concrete customer/owner examples. They explain the key distinctions before drawing conclusions: capacity versus purchases, nominal versus real GDP, owner income versus consumption, investment versus financing, and production versus welfare. The numerical examples are visibly invented. The capital-only endpoint is explicitly hypothetical, and the transition model does not claim a predicted bankruptcy date.

No remaining substantive economic or source blocker was identified in the reviewed snapshot. This is not independent validation of the interview numbers or the paid SemiAnalysis article: those passages were checked against the parent's timestamped research notes and public primary corroboration of the economic concepts. The draft correctly preserves uncertainty about the interview's revenue basis rather than turning it into a precise forecast.

## Issues resolved during review

- **Unsupported spreadsheet workflow claim, Part 1 line 15:** the text now says the town was imagined small enough to fit in a spreadsheet and modeled in code. That matches the available artifacts without inventing a spreadsheet-building step.
- **Trade surplus versus net lending, Part 2 line 67:** the passage now distinguishes the current account from the trade balance, includes the capital-account qualification, identifies both acquiring foreign assets and reducing foreign debt, and links [BEA's definitions](https://www.bea.gov/news/us-international-transactions-and-investment-position-release-additional-information).
- **Plain-language forecast terminology, Part 2 lines 101–105:** the first spending figure now says US capital spending and identifies chips, data centers and the wider supply chain. The revenue passage explains full-year revenue versus a December pace expressed as a year's worth. The $11.3 trillion estimate remains explicitly cumulative for 2024–2029 and distinct from a single year's US spending.
- **Source relevance and prose flow for measurement and the production boundary, Part 2 line 113:** the OECD and BEA Chapter 2 links now attach directly to the claims they support. BEA's value-added FAQ supports the distinct business-input consolidation point. The text correctly describes service measurement as difficult rather than impossible.

## Claims checked and worth preserving

- The BLS comparison uses one 2026 release vintage and a defined geography/sector: US nonfarm business labor compensation share, 62.8% in 2000 Q4 versus 52.8% in 2026 Q2. The ten-percentage-point decline is not presented as AI's historical effect. The article separately explains that a falling share need not mean falling labor dollars.
- The China passage uses the IMF consultation for weak domestic demand and the recommendation to rebalance toward consumption. It explicitly retains China's consumers, foreign sector, government and finance. It does not use China as evidence that a closed economy with zero consumption is sustainable or desirable.
- The domestic HR example holds final receipts fixed and consolidates the supplier chain. It does not confuse an avoided intermediate invoice with a corresponding aggregate GDP loss. The household production-boundary example is treated as a different event.
- The capital-only example gives owners both a consumption demand and an investment demand, makes maintenance explicit, and labels constant productivity, scalable nonlabor inputs and price clearing as assumptions. The all-investment example is a mathematical path with zero consumption, not an optimizing equilibrium or welfare recommendation.
- The original article already distinguished planned demand from equilibrium GDP and GDP growth from broad prosperity. The new drafts correctly present the town as a way to make those distinctions concrete, rather than claiming to overturn an error the original had already corrected.
