Model specification

The original baseline is specified below. The subsequent shock specification defines the scheduled changes, investment feedback, separate choice game and stationary maxima.

This is an uncalibrated thought experiment. All numerical inputs, technologies, spending rules, initial balances and payment priorities are author assumptions. The model predicts neither a country's GDP level nor a transition's duration. One period has no assigned calendar length. Values are nominal currency units per period; real values divide by a uniform price index, initially one.

The modules answer different questions. The simultaneous closure gives a comparative static. The input-output accounts explain production accounting. The cash ledger specifies a possible transition, with its own timing and financing assumptions. They should not be mistaken for interchangeable solutions to a complete general equilibrium model.

1. Simultaneous income and expenditure

solve_equilibrium(ClosureParameters(...)) returns a frozen result dataclass. Its .accounts is absent when the assumptions do not identify a feasible stationary output. dataclasses.asdict converts every public result to deterministic JSON-compatible structures; no random draws are used.

Let N be employed workers, w the nominal wage per worker, W=Nw, and T a transfer from owners to the worker household. Workers include unemployed people; they can receive transfers and ownership dividends even when N=0. At this layer there is no depreciation, government, trade, price adjustment, inventory accumulation, credit constraint or capital accumulation. Gross surplus is P=Y-W. Investment I is an actual purchase of newly produced domestic capital goods, financed by assumption; it is not a financial asset purchase.

Worker and owner desired consumption are

Cw = cw(W+T)
Co = co(Y-W-T)
Y = Cw + Co + I
Y* = [(cw-co)(W+T) + I] / (1-co), for co < 1.

The two households' disposable incomes sum to Y. Transfers reallocate income and do not themselves add GDP. The solution requires Y >= W+T: the owner household cannot fund a transfer from negative income. infeasible_income reports cases requiring a loss-financing or wealth-transfer rule absent here.

The baseline N=10, w=6, cw=1, co=0.5, I=20 gives W=60, P=40, Cw=60, Co=20 and Y=100. Losing L jobs at these parameters reduces the unconstrained stationary Y by 6L. This is the result of these spending assumptions, rather than an identity relating employment and GDP.

When co=1, the equation becomes 0=(cw-1)(W+T)+I. If its right side is zero, the model returns indeterminate; it selects no particular GDP from the continuum compatible with available capacity and nonnegative income. If the right side is nonzero, it returns no_stationary_equilibrium. In particular, cw=co=1 and I=0 does not imply collapse; cw=co=1 and I>0 cannot have a finite stationary solution under this closure because aggregate saving is zero.

Production is limited by nominal capacity pQ. If Y*>pQ, output is capped, investment gets first claim on it, and remaining consumption is allocated in proportion to the households' desired consumption. Unfilled orders are reported. This rationing convention supplies the missing short-run allocation rule; it does not solve for market-clearing prices. If capped output cannot cover wages and transfers, the case is infeasible rather than silently financed.

Completed accounts reconcile

nominal GDP = actual C + actual I = wages + gross surplus
gross saving = disposable income - actual C = actual I
real GDP = nominal GDP / p.

A lower p buys more real goods from unchanged nominal expenditure if physical capacity permits. A higher Q increases the ceiling on production; it does not increase desired nominal expenditure by itself.

investment_for_target(target_nominal_gdp, parameters) calculates

I_needed = max(0, (1-co)Y_target - (cw-co)(W+T)).

It rejects targets exceeding capacity or below wages plus transfer. In the baseline, keeping Y>=100 after L layoffs needs I>=20+3L. This is necessary spending arithmetic for the stated closure, not an assurance that good capital projects, finance, capacity, or ultimate consumption demand exist. The targeting helper rejects co=1 because that boundary does not identify a unique stationary output; the separate equilibrium result handles its continuum.

2. Ten firms and intermediate production

allocate_production(C, I, wage_bill=W, employed_workers=N, price=p) traces five sectors with two firms in each. Firms split their sector's business equally. No market power or competitive pricing mechanism is modeled.

The sectors are raw materials, processing, intermediates/services, consumption goods, and capital goods. The monetary input-coefficient matrix A has supplier rows and purchaser columns. A[i,j] is input i bought per unit of sector j's gross nominal output. The matrix is an explicit acyclic illustrative technology, not an empirical input-output table:

Supplier / buyer Raw Processing Services Consumption Capital
Raw 0 .40 .10 0 0
Processing 0 0 .20 .20 .20
Services 0 0 0 .30 .30
Consumption 0 0 0 0 0
Capital 0 0 0 0 0

With f=(0,0,0,C,I), gross output satisfies x=Ax+f. Reverse substitution solves this acyclic system without a numerical dependency. Each supplier's sales to each buyer are explicitly recorded. Every firm reports gross turnover, intermediate expenses, value added, wages and gross surplus.

At C=80 and I=20, gross turnover=169.4, intermediate expenses=69.4, and GDP=100. Adding firms' sales without deducting intermediate purchases would double count the production chain.

Worker time is shared across firms in proportion to value added. Firm worker counts are therefore worker-equivalents, not distinct people; the economy still has ten workers at the baseline, each earning six overall. Wages are allocated using the same weights. Baseline wage bills for each of the two firms in raw/processing/services/consumption/capital are 4.02/4.68/6.30/12/3. This avoids assigning six of wages to a firm with only five of value added. At zero output, any contracted wages are allocated equally. Losses require allow_losses=True, used only by the cash-constrained transition.

The common price index changes real quantities consistently. Relative input prices and physical substitution are not modeled. Sector production remains possible after layoffs only because an exogenous technology/capacity assumption permits it; this model does not derive robots or software from unemployed labor.

3. Cash payments and productive capital over time

simulate(DynamicsParameters(...)) returns a sequence of PeriodResult records, every actual payment, and opening/closing balances. This is a cash-conserving transaction ledger with an aggregate productive-capital stock, not complete national balance sheets: equity valuations, bank liabilities, loans, interest, land, inventories and financial-market prices are absent.

The initial 200 units of existing money are assigned to workers (60), owners (40), and ten firms (10 each). The genesis of this inherited stock is outside the experiment. The capital purchasing agent starts and ends each period with zero. Transfers only move money between registered accounts; overdrafts are forbidden. Every executed payment preserves the total stock of money.

Spending depends on last period's received cash income, subject to the spender's available cash. Initial lagged incomes are explicit inputs rather than claims that period-zero income has already been produced. Ownership share alpha sends alpha of dividends to workers; it is an income-right assumption, not an explicit asset-transfer transaction.

Each period follows this convention:

  1. Owners make the requested budget-balanced transfer, limited by their cash. Any shortfall is reported.
  2. Desired investment is max(0, I0 + beta*(previous_utilization-u_target)*K*p). Default beta=0 gives fixed desired investment. When enabled, owners pay available cash up to this amount as equal equity contributions to firms. Contributions finance investment but are not GDP. Each firm budgets the smaller of I_desired/10 and its current cash for capital purchases.
  3. Workers plan cw(lagged wages+lagged worker dividends+current transfer). Owners plan comax(0, lagged owner dividends-current transfer) plus optional owner_autonomous_consumption. Each plan is capped by cash after steps 1–2. The autonomous addition is spending that can recirculate as dividends, not necessarily a finite wealth drawdown.
  4. Physical capacity is paK. Investment receives first claim; consumption uses remaining capacity and is rationed proportionally. Firms fund a common capital purchasing agent, which pays the two capital producers equally. Households pay the two consumption-goods firms equally.
  5. Intermediate purchases settle from downstream buyers to upstream suppliers. Firms then pay as much of contracted wages as cash permits and distribute nonnegative gross surplus as dividends, capped by available cash. Ownership share determines the recipients. A payout includes the gross capital charge; replacement must be funded explicitly in step 2 or from firm reserves.
  6. Capital evolves as K_next=(1-delta)K + actual_I/p. Purchased goods become productive next period. Net domestic product is Y-p*delta*K.

Default a=1, K=200, delta=.1 and I=20 maintain capacity200. The same desired investment does not mean indefinite growth: it exactly replaces depreciation at the baseline. If financing fails, capital can shrink. Conversely, cheaper capital goods or changed investment can grow the capital stock. Full automation does not eliminate replacement or promise maintenance-free machines.

Gross national accounts use accrued contracted wages, while cash-income spending uses wages actually paid. Any unpaid wages appear as wage_arrears; the simulation stops at that period with stopped_for_insolvency=True. It does not erase these claims, continue on hidden credit, resolve bankruptcy, or model recovery. Terminal accounts can show operating losses and negative net product; all cash balances remain nonnegative.

This convention yields an exactly stationary cash-financed baseline Y100, C80, I20: owner saving20 is explicitly recycled as investment equity20. Disabling that recycling drains firm cash by20 per period. After five periods the initial firm reserves are exhausted and desired investment20 becomes actual investment0, even though owners hold the missing money. Finance is therefore a separate constraint from desired expenditure.

With immediate full layoffs but inherited balances, first-period Y remains100, workers spend their finite inherited60 and finish with zero cash. Subsequent Y is70,55,47.5,..., approaching40 at the default parameters. Owner cash rises from40 to100 while the aggregate money stock stays200. The periods are model steps, not a prediction of how quickly any real economy adjusts.

Transfers and partial ownership can support demand by routing income to the high-spending household. Investment finance still comes from owners or firm cash in this implementation. At extreme worker ownership, a realistic equity funding arrangement might need workers to invest too; that extension is not implicitly supplied. Neither transfer policy nor dividends create money here.

4. What the model does not establish

The result changes with spending propensities, actual investment, ownership, transfers, cash timing, capacity and prices. It does not establish a universal GDP ceiling after layoffs. It contains no optimized consumer utility, firm investment valuation, monetary policy, international demand, demographic structure, equilibrium interest rate, endogenous labor productivity, empirical parameter estimation, or welfare ranking. Capacity/technology gains are assumptions; GDP is not a welfare measure.

The separate growth.py counterexample uses a different closure: actual investment is a fixed share of productive output. Its maintained demand, financing and capital-productivity assumptions should be read with that module and the article. It must not be substituted into the lagged-spending ledger without reconciling their financial and behavioral rules.

Unit tests verify the accounting identities, funding and capacity boundaries, zero/ten layoffs, full-spending indeterminacy, real versus nominal units, explicit insolvency, deterministic outputs, and replay of every cash payment. Those checks establish implementation consistency, not empirical validity.

5. Fully automated growth: a separate existence example

automated_growth(GrowthParameters(...)) assumes Y_real=a*K, I_real=s*Y_real, C_real=(1-s)*Y_real, and K_next=(1-delta)*K+I_real. It therefore gives the exact per-period growth rate g=s*a-delta. Positive consumption and capital growth together require delta/a < s < 1; such an investment share exists only if a > delta.

All capital belongs to the owner group. Zero wages mean zero worker income and consumption here. A constant nominal expenditure flow E purchases all output at the assumed clearing price p=E/Y_real. E is a spending flow, not a specified stock of money. This module does not replay those purchases in the separate cash ledger. Price adjustment, scalable automated maintenance, non-scarce complements, constant capital productivity and willing owner demand are maintained assumptions, not findings about current AI.

Default K200, a0.5, s0.3, delta0.1 and E100 imply real GDP100, consumption70, replacement20, net capital addition10 and growth5%. After twenty transitions, GDP is265.3298 and consumption185.7308. Investing only20% replaces depreciation; investing100% produces growing GDP but zero consumption. Neither result is a welfare recommendation or an optimized household choice.

Domain boundary: full depreciation with no investment exhausts capital after one period. Requesting a later zero-output period raises ValueError, because no positive price can clear positive nominal spending against zero output. Likewise, numerical overflow or underflow in extreme long paths is not a valid economic result. The four named growth paths remain strictly positive and finite.
